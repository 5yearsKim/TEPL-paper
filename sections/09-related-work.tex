\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Declarative rewrite languages.}
MLIR's declarative rewrite rules (DRR) and PDLL simplify compiler rewrite
development by letting authors describe matching and replacement patterns
declaratively. DRR generates C++ implementations from TableGen
definitions~\cite{mlir-drr}, while PDLL provides a dedicated pattern language
with custom constraints and replacement logic~\cite{mlir-pdll}. These
systems focus on expressing and implementing rewrites over MLIR operations.
TEPL has a different focus: expressing and reasoning about tensor rewrites
through operation semantics. It brings together semantics for tensors of
arbitrary rank, applicability conditions, and attribute transformations in
one language.

\paragraph{Tensor rewrite verification.}
TensorRight verifies tensor graph rewrites for inputs of arbitrary rank and
size~\cite{tensorright}. Authors specify both sides of a rewrite and its
preconditions. TensorRight uses \emph{aggregated axes} to represent axis
groups and proves that SMT verification up to a finite rank bound suffices
to establish correctness for arbitrary ranks.

TensorRight verifies a rewrite whose axis relationships are supplied.
In contrast, TEPL accepts a specified RHS operation graph with missing
axis fields and synthesizes programs that compute them from source
metadata. This requires determining intermediate axes, satisfying
output-order constraints, and translating axis correspondences into
operand positions. TEPL then verifies the completed rewrite.

The systems also address different completeness questions. TensorRight
establishes when bounded-rank verification suffices to verify a supplied
rewrite for arbitrary ranks. TEPL's constructive completeness theorem
establishes when synthesis can find correct RHS attributes, under the stated
restrictions and assumptions.

\paragraph{Synthesis from semantics.}
Sketch lets authors write partial programs with holes, which a synthesizer
fills to satisfy a semantic specification~\cite{sketch}. TEPL follows this
idea by letting authors specify a tensor rewrite's operation structure while
delegating missing attribute transformation logic to the compiler. Related
synthesis approaches include finding programs within a specified grammar
in SyGuS~\cite{sygus}, composing library components to synthesize loop-free
programs~\cite{loop-free-synthesis}, and inferring rewrite rules through
equality saturation in Ruler~\cite{ruler}.
