\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paperparagraph{Tensor rewrite verification.}
TensorRight is the closest work: it verifies tensor graph rewrites for
arbitrary rank and size, proving that SMT checks up to a finite rank bound
suffice~\cite{tensorright}. Rule writers specify both sides of a rewrite and
its preconditions, and TensorRight checks them, using unordered named axes to
abstract away axis order. TEPL addresses the complementary problem of
\emph{constructing} the rewrite's missing pieces. Given a fixed RHS operation
graph, it synthesizes the attributes that make the RHS legal and equivalent
to the LHS. Many of these attributes are positional: a dot operation, for
example, needs contraction lists stating which axes to pair across its
operands. TEPL therefore keeps axis order through ordered axis groups and
symbolic axis mapping. The completeness questions differ accordingly.
TensorRight establishes when bounded-rank verification suffices for a
supplied rewrite, while TEPL establishes that synthesis decides whether valid
RHS attributes exist and constructs them whenever they do, under the stated
assumptions.

\paperparagraph{Rewrite languages, representations, and synthesis.}
Declarative rewrite systems such as MLIR's DRR and
PDLL~\cite{mlir-drr,mlir-pdll} specify matching and replacement patterns, and
can reuse matched attributes on the RHS. When a new attribute must be computed
from the match, however, the rule writer supplies it as native C++ (e.g.,
DRR's \code{NativeCodeCall} or PDLL's native rewrites), and neither system
checks that the result is defined, legal, or equivalent to the LHS. This is
precisely the attribute logic TEPL synthesizes. Alive verifies LLVM peephole
optimizations but likewise leaves the RHS to the rule writer~\cite{alive};
Alive-Infer infers preconditions rather than the replacement
itself~\cite{alive-infer}. Glenside and SPORES give tensor programs
representations that support rewriting~\cite{glenside,spores}, but neither
constructs the attributes of a fixed replacement graph. General synthesis
frameworks~\cite{sketch,sygus,loop-free-synthesis} and rule-discovery systems
such as Ruler, Halide rule synthesis, and Axon~\cite{ruler,halide-rules,axon}
search for programs or rules from semantic constraints. TEPL instead targets
one specific construction problem: supplying the rank-dependent positional
attributes of a given RHS graph, with a completeness guarantee for the stated
fragment.
